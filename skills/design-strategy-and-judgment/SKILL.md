# Design Strategy and Judgment

## Purpose

Make the Studio's design taste an active decision-making capability, not a polish pass added after implementation. Decide **what the website needs to communicate, which visual choices genuinely help, and which attractive patterns should be rejected**.

A strong design is not merely modern, polished or visually unusual. It makes the right business and visitor priorities unmistakable, then expresses them with a coherent visual point of view.

This skill produces a short direction in the project conversation. It does not require a separate strategy document, moodboard, wireframe package or client design questionnaire.

## When to use

Activate before establishing or materially changing a website's visual direction. Pair it with:

- `website-reference-discovery` when useful live references have not yet been selected;
- `ux-review` when the visitor journey or offer needs deeper examination;
- `frontend-design` for distinctive visual art direction;
- `typography-and-legibility` when type choices or readability need focused work.

## Non-negotiable principles

1. **Business truth before styling.** Never design around an invented offer, differentiator, claim, price, review, guarantee, contact or proof point.
2. **Visitor clarity before novelty.** Distinctiveness must improve recognition, comprehension, trust or action—not compete with them.
3. **Judge before borrowing.** A reference is evidence to inspect, not an instruction to imitate.
4. **One coherent direction.** Synthesize the strongest applicable ideas into one decisive design direction. Do not return routine font, color or layout choices to the owner.
5. **The hero earns its space.** The first screen must communicate the offer and make the next action obvious. Decoration cannot substitute for a business-specific visual.
6. **Show uncertainty honestly.** Mark facts as verified, client-provided/unverified, synthetic, assumed, missing or unknown as appropriate.

## 1. Understand the business decision

Use the project conversation and source material already available. Identify, briefly:

- **Business and offer:** what is actually being sold or provided?
- **Primary visitor:** who is most likely to arrive, and what are they trying to solve?
- **Primary decision:** what must the visitor understand or believe before acting?
- **Value:** what concrete outcome, convenience or experience matters most to that visitor?
- **Differentiator:** what genuinely distinguishes this offer, if evidence supports one?
- **Trust:** what proof or operational detail can be shown honestly?
- **Friction:** what question, uncertainty or perceived risk could stop the visitor?
- **Primary action:** what should happen next, and what verified/approved destination or real interaction supports it?

Do not invent a differentiator simply to make the copy sound stronger. When no defensible differentiator or social proof exists, use clarity about the offer, scope, process, pricing, location, timing or next step instead.

Keep the working conclusion compact: one sentence for the visitor and one sentence for the main business outcome the page must support.

## 2. Define the visual job

Choose the page's dominant message and the strongest visual expression of the actual business.

Before selecting colors or fonts, decide:

- What should a first-time visitor understand within roughly five seconds?
- What is the one most important thing they should notice first?
- Which real product, service, place, person-at-work, material, result or interaction best makes the offer tangible?
- What should the primary CTA say, and exactly what happens after it is used?
- What evidence or helpful detail should sit near the first action?
- What can be removed because it does not help recognition, understanding, trust or action?

### Hero standard

The hero is the highest-priority sales and comprehension surface, not merely a decorative opening section. It should normally make these elements immediately clear:

1. **Offer and audience:** what the business does and who it helps, in understandable words.
2. **Benefit:** the concrete outcome or reason to care, without unsupported superlatives.
3. **Useful supporting detail:** a real inclusion, price cue, process, location, timing or other decision detail when available.
4. **Primary action:** a direct, specific CTA with a real approved destination/action in `client_service`, or an unmistakably synthetic/local action in `studio_development`.
5. **Business-specific visual:** an intentional image, product/service scene, original illustration, demonstration or other visual that makes the offer more tangible.
6. **Trust cue:** genuine proof or useful operational clarity close to the action, when supported.

Do not force all six into equal-size blocks. Establish a clear hierarchy and let the most important content dominate.

Do not settle for a text-only hero, generic gradient, abstract blob or unrelated stock photo merely because it is quick to implement. Use the actual approved business imagery where available. Otherwise choose an appropriate original/authorized asset or state the material asset gap. In `client_service`, do not imply that generated concept imagery depicts actual staff, premises, customers or completed work. In `studio_development`, synthetic visual assets are allowed but remain demo-only.

A CTA is not complete because it looks like a button. Confirm its exact destination and post-click behavior. Never invent a phone number, WhatsApp contact, booking system, form submission or operational workflow.

## 3. Judge references with taste

Use no more than three genuinely useful references for one direction. Inspect the actual rendered page and responsive states before making visual claims. A search snippet, award, popularity, polished mockup or attractive screenshot alone does not prove that a reference is effective.

Judge each candidate against the following dimensions:

| Dimension | Questions |
|---|---|
| Business and audience fit | Does the design suit the category, customer expectations, price/positioning and real visitor task? |
| First-screen clarity | Can a new visitor quickly tell what is offered, why it matters and what to do next? |
| Hero strength | Is the headline specific, the image relevant and well-composed, and the CTA obvious? |
| Visual hierarchy | Is attention guided deliberately, with a clear dominant element and useful section rhythm? |
| Craft | Are typography, spacing, alignment, proportion, color, contrast and detail handled with control? |
| Imagery | Does the imagery feel credible, distinctive and useful, with deliberate crop, scale and focal point? |
| Conversion and trust | Does the page answer decision questions, reduce friction and provide a clear next action without unsupported claims? |
| Responsive quality | Does the design remain coherent and usable on mobile, not merely shrink to fit? |
| Originality | Is there a meaningful point of view, or is it mostly a familiar template with a new palette? |

For every candidate, make one concise decision:

- **KEEP** — a strong pattern that fits the business and visitor task with little change.
- **ADAPT** — a useful idea that needs substantial adjustment to fit the project's audience, content or brand.
- **REJECT** — visually weak, confusing, generic, inaccessible, poorly responsive, misleading or irrelevant to this project.

Explain the decision with observed evidence, not vague praise such as "looks premium" or "modern". Name the exact pattern worth keeping or rejecting: hero composition, imagery, CTA placement, pricing presentation, type hierarchy, navigation, proof, spacing rhythm or mobile behavior.

Separate **visual craftsmanship** from **business effectiveness**. A portfolio concept may have excellent art direction but no proven conversion behavior. A real business site may have a useful visitor journey but weak visual execution. Do not claim conversion performance unless reliable performance data is available.

Do not adopt a pattern solely because it is popular, trendy, attractive in isolation or easy to reproduce. Reject attractive patterns when they make the offer harder to understand, bury the CTA, introduce visual noise, weaken readability or do not fit the actual audience.

## 4. Synthesize one art direction

Combine useful patterns at the level of principles and components—not by copying an entire website. One reference may contribute the hero composition, another the way it explains pricing, and another a clear service journey.

State one recommended direction in a compact handoff:

- **Design concept:** one sentence describing the visual personality and why it fits this business.
- **Visual hierarchy:** what leads, supports and recedes.
- **Hero:** headline direction, supporting detail, image subject/composition, primary CTA and any secondary action.
- **Image direction:** required subject, mood, crop, focal point, asset source and rights/permission status.
- **Typography:** deliberate type roles and a clear hierarchy, not just fashionable font names.
- **Color and surfaces:** a restrained palette with a reason for each role.
- **Layout and rhythm:** alignment, density, section transitions and responsive behavior.
- **Reference decisions:** what to keep, adapt and reject, and why.
- **Risks/unknowns:** only material facts or assets that could change the result.

Use the smallest useful set of design tokens. Do not spend time inventing elaborate token systems before the visual direction is sound.

## 5. Challenge the direction before coding

Run these checks and revise the direction if it fails:

- **Five-second check:** can a first-time visitor identify the business, offer and next step from the first screen?
- **Hero test:** is the main visual specific to the business and does it strengthen the message? Would removing it materially weaken the page? If not, it may be decoration.
- **Action test:** is the CTA clear, visible and supported by a real action?
- **Reference test:** can each borrowed pattern be tied to an observed strength and a visitor need?
- **Originality test:** could this direction be dropped unchanged onto several unrelated businesses? If yes, it is not specific enough.
- **Restraint test:** what should be removed to make the dominant message and visual stronger?
- **Mobile test:** does the same message remain clear and compelling when the layout stacks?

If the direction fails, revise it before implementation. Do not rely on repeated post-build polish to rescue a strategically weak concept.

## 6. Handoff and working style

Give the implementer only what is needed to execute the selected direction accurately. Keep the summary in the project conversation unless a file materially improves handoff or reproducibility.

Do not present several equally weighted design routes unless the owner explicitly asks for alternatives. Make the recommendation, state its rationale and proceed autonomously within scope.

Ask the owner only when a missing fact, asset right, permission, public claim, destination, material scope decision or approval genuinely blocks safe progress. Do not ask the owner to choose routine colors, fonts, spacing, component shapes or layout details.

This skill does not authorize copying protected work, inventing business evidence, changing scope, publishing a development demo or performing consequential external actions.
